Ionnoioni

Writing System: Siddham, Simplified by Design

Ionnoioni is written using letters borrowed from the historical Siddham script. You do not need to learn Siddham itself to read or write Ionnoioni, only the small, closed set of rules below.

Why only a piece of Siddham

Siddham is an abugida: a consonant letter already carries a built-in vowel, and other vowels attach as small marks rather than standing as separate letters. Ionnoioni has only six consonants and five vowels, a fraction of Sanskrit's sound system, so it needs only a handful of consistent mutation rules, applied every time, not a large memorized inventory of shapes.

The rules

  1. A bare consonant carries "a" by default. 𑖎 is ka. No mark needed.
  2. R is always a trill. Think of the Spanish rr.
  3. Other vowels attach as small marks (matras): e, i, o, u, ai and au. One glyph, one syllable.
  4. A vowel with no consonant before it stands alone in full form: at the start of a word or after another vowel.
  5. A consonant with no vowel after it takes a virama, which silences its built-in "a." That happens at the end of a word (kaun, 𑖎𑖻𑖡𑖿), inside a cluster (kri, 𑖎𑖿𑖨𑖰) and before oi. The test is always the same: if the "a" is heard, write the bare consonant; if no vowel is heard, add the virama.
  6. oi is the one exception. It is always written as its own full glyph, 𑖃, borrowed from Sanskrit's otherwise unused long ī, and never as a mark. A consonant before it takes a virama, so koi is 𑖎𑖿𑖃 and oiroi is 𑖃𑖨𑖿𑖃.
  7. Vowel pairs never split. o followed by i is always oi, a followed by i is always ai and a followed by u is always au, so aio is 𑖋𑖌. Other vowel meetings are written as they sound: ia is 𑖂𑖀, and a true double i is two glyphs, as in tuiion, 𑖝𑖲𑖂𑖂𑖌𑖡𑖿.

Numerals are written as ordinary Devanagari digits, spoken with their own words. Direction is left to right, in a plain line, with spaces between words and no special stacking convention.

A note on nr, tr and pr

Three particles break the rule that every spoken vowel is written. The intimacy particle nr, the voluntary particle tr and the involuntary particle pr are each written as a fused pair, a consonant linked to r by virama, and stop there: 𑖡𑖿𑖨, 𑖝𑖿𑖨 and 𑖢𑖿𑖨. No vowel is ever written after them, though each is spoken with a soft, unwritten [ɛ]: [nrɛ], [trɛ], [prɛ]. For nr the gap between what is written and what is spoken is the point: it marks something confided between speaker and listener, never fully committed to the page. Its meaning also shifts by position, opening a clause, closing one or bracketing both ends. Because the final r carries no virama, tr and pr look identical on the page to the verbs tra (be comfortable) and pra (speak); context tells them apart.

Independent Vowels

Used word-initially, or whenever a vowel follows another vowel rather than a consonant.

aeiouaiauoi
𑖀𑖊𑖂𑖌𑖄𑖋𑖍𑖃

oi borrows its form from Sanskrit's otherwise unused long ī. It has no vowel mark; after a consonant it is still written in full (see the table below).

A tool worth knowing about

Aksharamukha is a free online script-conversion tool that supports Siddham directly among dozens of Indic scripts. It can help check or generate matra placement, virama clusters and independent-vowel forms when writing longer Ionnoioni text, since it applies real Sanskrit orthographic convention automatically. It has no knowledge of Ionnoioni itself and treats the input as Sanskrit, so ordinary consonant-vowel combinations come out right but oi needs a little help:

  • Type oi as ii, which gives the glyph 𑖃 at the start of a word or after a vowel.
  • After a consonant the tool writes ii as a vowel mark (𑖱). Replace that mark with a virama followed by 𑖃, so 𑖎𑖱 becomes 𑖎𑖿𑖃 (koi).
  • A real double i, as in tuiion, must be typed with the two i's separated (tu i i on), or the tool will turn them into oi.

Hearing it, too

The alphabet song is called pipsa (𑖢𑖰𑖢𑖿𑖭), The Basis. Whoever can recite The Basis can sing, make poetry, write and keep a record.
