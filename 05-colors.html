kris: Colors

Ionnoioni names colors the way a painter mixes them, not the way a field guide names them. There is no word that means "the color of the sky" or "the color of blood." Instead there is a small palette of root colors and a handful of rules for blending them, softening them and pushing them, so that a speaker can name what they actually see rather than reach for a fixed list. The aim is not a complete catalog of every shade, but a set of tools.

The color class

Every color word ends in -s, written 𑖭𑖿 in Siddham, in the same way most numerals end in -n. A simple color word has the shape consonant, vowel, s. Color, like texture, is a way of qualifying how the world is experienced, and the shared s is a quiet nod to the S class.

Color words behave like any other descriptive word. They can stand on their own as a zero-copula predicate or attach to a noun with i.

kris: the word for color

The word for color itself is kris, 𑖎𑖿𑖨𑖰𑖭𑖿, built from kri (see) and the class marker -s: the seen, taken as color. It carries the -s of the class it names but opens with a cluster, so it belongs to the color words without being mistaken for one of them.

The question word ka kio comes first when asking which color. A lone final ka asks a yes or no question instead.

The root palette

Five colors are roots: the three painter's primaries plus white and black. Everything else is built from them.

Blended colors

A color halfway between two others takes the consonant of one parent and the vowel of the other. The vowel also tracks lightness: i is light, e sits in the middle and u is dark, which is why grey falls exactly between white and black.

Ambivalent colors

Some colors hover between two others without belonging to either: teal, vermilion, magenta. These take two syllables, the first syllable of one parent followed by the whole of the other. As everywhere else in the language, the lead element grounds the word and the trailing element qualifies it, so rores is a red leaning toward orange, while reros is an orange leaning toward red.

Lightness and saturation: ta and ra

The softener ta and the intensifier ra already mean something different depending on where they stand: in front of a word they mark how a thing arrives, after it they mark how it builds. Colors borrow that same logic. In front, they describe how hard the color strikes the eye, its saturation. After, they describe how deep the color goes, its lightness. On both sides, they describe the whole color.

This is meant as a springboard rather than a fixed chart. The English labels below are rough neighbors, not definitions, and the three hues are only a starting point.

ta and ra never mix. That rule holds here as everywhere else, so a form like ra ros ta does not exist. A color that is both vivid and pale, a neon pastel, is rare enough that the language reaches it another way: by mixing with white, below.

Tint, tone and shade: mixing with tis, tes and tus

A second route to lightness follows the painter's own terms. Attach white, grey or black with i, and the color is mixed with it. This sits comfortably alongside ta and ra, and covers combinations they cannot.

Warmth and coolness

Ionnoioni has no dedicated words for warm and cool, and it does not need to coin any. Two existing tools do the work, and they can be used together.

Leaning toward a neighbor with o

The particle o means "toward," and a color can lean toward any neighbor on the wheel. This is the precise option, since it names exactly which way a color drifts.

PhraseSiddhamReads as
kus o kes𑖎𑖲𑖭𑖿 𑖌 𑖎𑖸𑖭𑖿green leaning yellow, a warm green
kus o nus𑖎𑖲𑖭𑖿 𑖌 𑖡𑖲𑖭𑖿green leaning blue, a cool green
nus o nos𑖡𑖲𑖭𑖿 𑖌 𑖡𑖺𑖭𑖿blue leaning violet
ros o kes𑖨𑖺𑖭𑖿 𑖌 𑖎𑖸𑖭𑖿red leaning yellow, a warm red

Fire and water

For a general sense of temperature, attach kso (fire) for warmth or tsu (water) for coolness with i. These are not color words themselves, only qualifiers on a color, so the palette still holds to its rule of not naming colors after things in nature. Because they are ordinary words, ta and ra can scale them.

Use o when the direction matters and kso or tsu when only the temperature does.

Color and feeling

Colors carry feeling, and not only in poetry. A large study by Jonauskaite and colleagues asked thousands of people across thirty countries which emotions they linked with which colors, and found strong patterns shared across cultures: red with both love and anger, pink with love and pleasure, yellow with joy, white with hope and relief, blue with calm, grey with disappointment and black with sadness. They also found that lightness, saturation and warmth shift those feelings in systematic ways, which is part of why Ionnoioni gives speakers tools for exactly those three.

None of these associations is built into the words themselves. A ros is simply red. But the palette was shaped with this research in mind, and the associations are worth knowing, because color in Ionnoioni sits close to its rich vocabulary of mood and texture, and the two can be used to color each other.
