Ionnoioni

Grammar, Syntax & Mechanics

Ionnoioni is analytic: word order and a small set of invariant particles carry every grammatical relationship. No word ever changes its own shape to mark tense, case, number or possession, only the words placed around it do.

Word order & zero-copula predication

Basic order is Subject-Verb-Object. Predicates attach directly to a subject with no linking "to be" verb at all. This isn't limited to mood words, it applies to any predicate: a mood root, a descriptive/quality word or anything else standing in that slot. Mood was simply where the pattern first got demonstrated.

𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖎𑖿𑖨𑖰 𑖝𑖿𑖃 𑖭𑖿𑖡𑖸 𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖹 𑖨𑖸𑖋
nio kri toi sne nio prai reai
1SGsee.V2SG.OBJand1SGneed.Vlove.MOOD
I see you and I need love
𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖡𑖿𑖨𑖺𑖎𑖺
nio nroko
1SGstrange.ATTR
I am strange

A descriptive/quality word (ordinarily seen attaching to a noun via i, as in nroko i tsi, "strange tree") stands here as a bare predicate with no linking verb, exactly the way a mood word does.

Pronouns

Ionnoioni has three pronouns, each with a subject form and an object form. The consonant marks the person (n for the speaker, t for the one spoken to, k for anyone or anything else) and the order of the two vowels marks the role: io for the subject, oi for the object. The plural particle u in front makes any of them plural.

SubjectObjectSiddhamMeaning
nionoi𑖡𑖰𑖌 / 𑖡𑖿𑖃I / me
tiotoi𑖝𑖰𑖌 / 𑖝𑖿𑖃you / you
kiokoi𑖎𑖰𑖌 / 𑖎𑖿𑖃he, she or it / him, her or it
u niou noi𑖄 𑖡𑖰𑖌 / 𑖄 𑖡𑖿𑖃we / us
u tiou toi𑖄 𑖝𑖰𑖌 / 𑖄 𑖝𑖿𑖃you (more than one)
u kiou koi𑖄 𑖎𑖰𑖌 / 𑖄 𑖎𑖿𑖃they / them

kio carries no gender and no class: it is he, she and it alike, for people, animals and things. In this Ionnoioni follows languages like Turkish and Persian, whose third-person pronouns (Turkish o, Persian u) make no gender distinction either. Number, on the other hand, is always available through u.

Which form to use

The subject form is for the one doing or being: before the verb, or as the subject of a zero-copula predicate. It is also the form that takes the possessive e: nio e kea, my sibling. (For "your," both tio e and toi e are heard.)

The object form is for everything else: after the verb, as the one receiving the action, and after any particle at all, as in a noi (with me), o toi (toward you), na o koi (away from it) and a u noi (with us).

𑖎𑖰𑖌 𑖝𑖭𑖿 𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖺 𑖌 𑖡𑖿𑖃।
kio tas tro o noi.
3SGPSTwalk.Vtoward1SG.OBJ
She walked toward me.
𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖊 𑖎𑖸𑖀
nio e kea
1SGPOSSsibling
my sibling

The reflexive

There is no separate reflexive pronoun: the object form is simply doubled. Doubling is always on the object pronoun; doubling the subject pronoun is not a recognized construction.

𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖨𑖸𑖋 𑖡𑖿𑖃 𑖡𑖿𑖃
nio reai noi noi
1SGlove.MOOD1SG.OBJ1SG.OBJ
I love myself

This has a useful consequence. Because pointing back to the subject requires the doubled form, a single object pronoun never refers to its own subject. In kio kri koi, the one seeing and the one seen are two different people. The section on keeping track of who's who, below, puts this to work.

Particles

e: possessive, and reaching inside a kenning

𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖊 𑖋𑖊𑖭𑖿
nio e aies
1SGPOSSafterglow.MOOD
my afterglow

e's ordinary job is possession, but it has a second, more deliberate use: reaching inside a multi-root kenning and re-possessing just one inner piece of it, rather than claiming the whole compound. This is a genuinely different move from i's job below (binding a kenning as one indivisible unit); e can decompose it.

𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖊 𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖰𑖡𑖿 𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖺𑖡𑖿, 𑖊 𑖭𑖹𑖭𑖻 𑖡 𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖲
nio e prin tron, e saisau na pru
1SGPOSSsmall-dark(heart)POSSrhythmNEGknow.V
my heart, its rhythm is uncertain

Reserved for more creative or technical registers; the compact everyday form uses i instead (see Idioms, Metaphors & Kennings).

u: plural

𑖄 𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖨𑖸𑖋 𑖝𑖿𑖃
u nio reai toi
PL1SGlove.MOOD2SG.OBJ
we love you

u also has a bound, non-plural use inside compounds, marking immersion/containment the way a vowel-shift to -u does elsewhere (e.g. raeu, "threat-immersed place," from rae "enemy" + u). This is accepted homophony: free-standing u is always plural; bound u inside a compound carries containment instead.

i: general binding link

i's real job is broader than "attach a modifier to a noun." It binds any two units where bare juxtaposition alone would be ambiguous, numerals, descriptive roots, kinship depth, a kenning taken as one whole, even a pronoun bound to a bracketed primal-register cry. The modifier+noun pattern below was simply the first place it got demonstrated; it was never the limit of what i does.

𑖎𑖡𑖿 𑖂 𑖢𑖿𑖭

threeATTRstone
three stones
𑖝𑖡𑖿 𑖂 𑖝𑖸 𑖎𑖺𑖀
tan i te koa

great-grandparent
𑖢𑖨𑖿 𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖂 𑖨 𑖂𑖀 𑖨 । 𑖨 𑖝𑖿𑖨 𑖨 𑖭𑖿𑖡𑖸 𑖋𑖌
par nio i ra ia ra, ra tra ra sne aio
FUT1SGATTRINTENS[PRIMAL: surprise-burst]INTENSINTENScomfortable.VINTENSandjoy.PRIMAL
I thought I'd feel ecstatic (future-me, intense surprise-burst, intensely comfortable and joy)

Here i binds a pronoun directly to a bracketed primal-register cry, material i had never joined before. Once i's job is stated as "bind any two units where juxtaposition would be ambiguous," this follows from the existing rule rather than stretching it.

na: negation (item-scoped; a minimal pair)

𑖡 𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖲

to be uncertain
𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖲 𑖡

Same two words, opposite order, negation attaches to whatever it stands beside, not to the whole clause.

ka: question marker, by position

ka is the sixth positional particle, alongside nr, tr, pr, ra and ta: where it stands changes what kind of question is being asked. At the end of a sentence it asks an ordinary question. At the start, joined to a question word, it frames the whole sentence as a question from the outset (see Function words, below). Mid-sentence it flags "I ask you." At both ends, it holds the question open the whole way through, an insistent question.

𑖝𑖰𑖌 𑖎𑖿𑖨 𑖀 𑖡𑖿𑖃 𑖎
tio kra a noi ka
2SGbe.safe.Vwith1SG.OBJQ
Do you feel safe with me?

ka can also appear mid-sentence to flag "I ask you" before the question itself arrives, slowing the sentence down and signaling a genuine, open question rather than a rhetorical one.

𑖡𑖿𑖨, 𑖝𑖰𑖌 𑖨 𑖃 𑖨, 𑖭𑖿𑖨𑖸 𑖝𑖿𑖃 𑖎: 𑖃 𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖲𑖡𑖿 𑖝𑖰𑖌 𑖊 𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖲 𑖎?
nr, tio ra oi ra, sre toi ka: oi trun tio e pru ka?
INT(open)2SGINTENSalarm.MOODINTENSbut2SG.OBJQ(I ask)alarm.MOODcause.V2SGPOSSknow.VQ
Your alarm is intense throughout, but I ask you: does the alarm cause your knowing?
𑖎 𑖭𑖿𑖎𑖸 𑖝𑖰𑖌 𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖸 𑖎?
ka ske tio pre ka?
Qwhy2SGupset.VQ
Why are you upset? (insistent, the question held throughout)

a / o: relational particles (with / toward)

Already shown above: a marking "with" in tio kra a noi. Combined with negation, na a or na o yields "not with" / "away from," at no extra cost.

A pronoun after any relational particle takes its object form: a noi (with me), o toi (toward you), na o koi (away from it), a u noi (with us). The subject forms nio, tio and kio are kept for the one doing or being.

"Instead" / "instead of": ste + o recombined

Not built from negation: "instead" never denies the unchosen option exists, it only selects among live options, which is why ste (or) is the right anchor rather than na. An early attempt to frame this as exclusive-or logic (na ste) was tested and rejected, that reads as NOR (neither), the wrong shape entirely. Two orderings carry genuinely different weight, not interchangeable.

𑖭𑖿𑖝𑖸 𑖌
ste o
ortoward
instead? (forward-offering, e.g. "we have no coffee, tea instead?")
𑖌 𑖭𑖿𑖝𑖸
o ste
towardor
instead of (retrospective-asserting, e.g. "she walked instead of driving")

ta: de-intensifier, and the connectives sre / ske / spe

𑖝𑖰𑖌 𑖨𑖻𑖨𑖿 𑖎

𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖨𑖻𑖨𑖿 𑖝 𑖭𑖿𑖨𑖸 𑖡 𑖀 𑖝𑖿𑖃

𑖝 𑖂𑖀 𑖝

spe (if/then) bundles both halves of a conditional in one particle: a bare spe-clause followed immediately by a second clause reads as the complete "if X, then Y" with no second connective needed.

𑖭𑖿𑖢𑖸 𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖡 𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖸𑖎𑖿𑖨, 𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖺 𑖡 𑖌 𑖢𑖨𑖿 𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖺𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖰

The intimacy register, tr, and pr

nr is written 𑖡𑖿𑖨, spoken [nrɛ], and was the first particle whose meaning shifts by position rather than by presence alone. Two more particles now follow the same pattern: tr [trɛ] marking voluntary/self-originated action, and pr [prɛ] marking involuntary/happened-to action. Before these existed, nothing in the grammar marked whether an action was chosen or imposed; every verb was neutral on that axis by default. tr echoes tre (give), the self as the source; pr echoes par (future), something not yet authored by the subject, arriving at them rather than from them.

nr, opening only, inviting, tentative

𑖡𑖿𑖨 𑖝𑖰𑖌 𑖎𑖿𑖨 𑖀 𑖡𑖿𑖃 𑖎
nr tio kra a noi ka
INT(open)2SGbe.safe.Vwith1SG.OBJQ
Do you feel safe with me? (asked gently)

nr's scope is broad by design: it marks any offer of intimacy in the general sense, closeness, trust, vulnerability extended, not narrowly romantic or erotic. It applies as naturally to a therapeutic alliance or a close friendship as to romance.

nr, closing only, deferred, private-later

See nio kra ske tio trekra noi nr above, the answer closes with nr, marking something held rather than something newly offered.

nr, both ends, full intimate register

𑖡𑖿𑖨 𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖸𑖎𑖿𑖨𑖰 𑖝𑖿𑖃 𑖝𑖿𑖭𑖺 𑖨𑖸𑖋 𑖡𑖿𑖨 𑖎
nr nio trekri toi tso reai nr ka
INT1SGshow.V(give+see)2SG.OBJwaylove.MOODINTQ
How can I show you love?

tr and pr, voluntary / involuntary

𑖝𑖿𑖨 𑖝𑖿𑖭𑖺𑖨𑖿 𑖭𑖿𑖨𑖸 𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖲
tr tsor sre pru
VOLbelieve.Vbutknow.V
believing in spite of knowing (voluntary self-deception, read as one idiom)
𑖢𑖿𑖨 𑖡𑖝𑖲𑖂
pr natui
INVOLdisconnection.MOOD
involuntary delusion, a break in the mind's own reality-tracking

Not every word needs tr or pr stated; most default to one reading or the other based on how the experience typically unfolds, and the particle is only added when the distinction actually needs to be made explicit. A few words take neither: naireo (projection) deliberately marks no voluntariness at all, since the experience of being projected upon is identical regardless of the projector's intent, and marking the target's voluntariness would imply they had some choice in receiving it. tr/pr mark the boundary of their own usefulness as much as their reach.

Bracket particles, ra & ta on a single mood word

Unlike nr, which brackets a whole clause, ra (intensify) and ta (soften) bracket a single mood root, and their position marks where the intensity falls, onset, resolution or throughout. Delivery (speed, volume, pitch) completes the meaning further; this is never written down.

𑖃 𑖨

The primals

Beneath all of Ionnoioni's emotional vocabulary sit three primals, the first words the language had for feeling. Each is a single diphthong, and each names one of the basic emotional systems described by the neuroscientist Jaak Panksepp.

PrimalSiddhamMeaningPanksepp system
ai𑖋desireSEEKING
au𑖍painPANIC/GRIEF
oi𑖃alarmFEAR

All three are falling diphthongs: each starts on an open vowel and closes toward i or u. They are the sound of something tightening, which suits feelings that grip. The primals are mood words, so they stand directly on a subject with no linking verb, and they take the ta and ra brackets like any other word. They also lend their senses to the vowel slots of the S class, where ai, au and oi read as desire, pain and alarm.

𑖝𑖰𑖌 𑖋
tio ai
2SGdesire.MOOD
You are desire.
𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖨 𑖃 𑖨
nio ra oi ra
1SGINTENSalarm.MOODINTENS
I am alarm, intense throughout.

The children of the primals

Each primal has two children, made by placing i or u in front of it. The two vowels keep the senses they carry everywhere else in the language. i binds, so the i-child is focal: the feeling is pinned to one thing. u immerses, so the u-child is diffuse: the feeling has no edges and surrounds you.

oi, alarmioi: a startle at one thing, "yikes!"𑖂𑖃uoi: alarm with no source, dread all around, dismay𑖄𑖃

The children are mood words, just like their parents, and take the same brackets. They do not add new senses to the S class, whose eight vowel slots stay as they are.

iau was a cry in the Primal Register before it was recognized as a child of au; its meaning did not change. uau sounds like "wow," and Portuguese spells "wow" exactly that way. In the standard language it is the howl of pain, but a dialect that drifts it toward amazement would be doing something entirely natural.

The Primal Register

Unbroken runs of pure vowels, with no consonant, no virama, no bracket particle, no grammatical attachment of any kind. Not vocabulary, a register: the sound of feeling that has exceeded the mind's capacity to shape it into structure at all. Length and arc carry the weight, not which specific vowels appear or in what order, a short run reads as a sharp cry, still barely contained; a long, unbroken run reads as something closer to a wail, structure fully abandoned.

The register has two uses: standing alone as a complete, raw cry, or inserted mid-sentence as phonetic punctuation, a burst of unstructured feeling dropped at a natural break point, skipped over entirely by the surrounding grammar rather than joined to it. A bracketed primal cry (ta ia ta, ra ia ra) can also be bound to a noun or pronoun via i, as shown above.

𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖎𑖿𑖨𑖰 𑖊 𑖝𑖸 𑖎𑖺𑖀 𑖎𑖻𑖨𑖿 𑖃𑖃𑖍 𑖭𑖿𑖡𑖸 𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖡 𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖡𑖿 𑖎𑖿𑖃 𑖎𑖻𑖨𑖿 𑖡𑖿𑖨

I watched my grandfather die, oioiau, and I could not save him.

Opening cries

The primals and their children all close. A second family of cries does the opposite: these rising shapes start on i or u and open outward, the sound of something released. Rising shapes are what interjections are made of in language after language, and these are no exception: said quickly, many of them land close to familiar cries.

Ideophones, a fifth, exempt category

Ideophones are not built from the four root-class templates (animate, inanimate, verb, mood). Their shape imitates the sound they name, not a grammatical category, the same way kennings are exempt from the length ceiling and nr/tr/pr are exempt from sound-spelling correspondence. Sustained, repetitive or buzzing calls lean on the trill (r); short, breathy bursts stay minimal, often bare vowel-plus-consonant with no onset; calls with natural repetition may reduplicate.

Verbifying an ideophone

An ideophone can graduate into ordinary grammar by fusing a stop+r verb-marking onset directly in front of it, keeping the original mimetic sound fully audible inside the new word.

𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖎𑖿𑖨𑖎𑖿
nio krak
1SGlaugh.V(fused ak)
I laugh

Aspect (in principle)

Ordinary verbs (plain CCV) have no internal vowel-consonant-vowel slot to reframe the way mood templates do, so aspect on an ordinary verb is marked externally instead, by the same particles already built for other jobs: ta for an ongoing, not-yet-settled state, tas for a resolved one. No new mechanism is needed.

𑖝𑖰𑖌 𑖎𑖿𑖨
tio kra
2SGbe.safe.V
immediate, you are safe
𑖝𑖰𑖌 𑖎𑖿𑖨 𑖝
tio kra ta
2SGbe.safe.VDEG(soft)
ongoing, settling into safety, not yet fully there
𑖝𑖰𑖌 𑖎𑖿𑖨 𑖝𑖭𑖿
tio kra tas
2SGbe.safe.VPST
resolved, was safe; safety now stands as an accomplished state

Function words

Questions, time, possibility, reasons and comparison are all built from words and particles the language already had. Nothing on this list required a new root except nokotu (perhaps), and even that was worn down from existing words.

A question word is ka placed in front of the word that names what is being asked about. Because it stands at the start, it frames the sentence as a question from the outset.

Can, perhaps, should and must

Joining clauses: while, so that and therefore

The relational particles also join whole clauses. a (with) joins two things happening together, "while" or "when." o (toward) points a clause at its purpose, "so that." "Therefore" is ske o: ske (because) looks back from an effect to its cause, and adding o turns it to face forward, from the cause to what follows. It is the same move that made ste o (instead) out of ste (or).

English uses "so" for both of the last two. Ionnoioni keeps them apart: o is purpose, something done so that something else will happen, while ske o is consequence, something true from which something else follows. With ka ske (why), the set is complete: ka ske asks for the cause, ske gives it and ske o draws the conclusion.

The left-binding rule

Several fixed pairs end in o: tas o (still), tse o (there), ste o (instead), na o (from), ka o (what for) and ske o (therefore). In every one of them, o belongs to the word before it, and two pairs never share an o. So a run like nio kro tas o, ske o ... can only be heard one way: still, therefore. The one older exception is o ste (instead of), which looks back over the unchosen option and binds forward.

"More" is pri (increase) placed before the quality, and the standard of comparison follows na o (away from), taking the object form like any pronoun after a particle.

Level, above and below

The direction words te (up), se (level) and pe (down) give comparison a picture to stand on. se followed by a standard means "as ... as," level with it, and the standard takes the object form as it does after any particle.

𑖝𑖰𑖌 𑖎𑖿𑖨𑖸 𑖭𑖸 𑖡𑖿𑖃
tio kre se noi
2SGgoodlevel1SG.OBJ
You are as good as me.

With no standard named, a direction word measures against the level that is expected: the circumstances, all else being equal. te is above that level, se is at it and pe is below it. Where English needs a whole clause, "under the circumstances" or "relatively speaking," Ionnoioni points to the baseline with a single word.

The two kinds of comparison divide the work. pri and prin measure more or less of a quality against a named standard. te, se and pe place something against a level, the named one when there is one and the expected one when there is not.

A bare numeral, with no following noun, can stand alone when the referent is recoverable from context, functioning much like English "both" standing in for two already-mentioned things. Keeping track of who's who

Because kio has no gender, a story with several people in it cannot lean on he and she the way English does. Ionnoioni keeps track instead with four tools, two of which come straight out of rules already described above.

  1. The topic default. A bare kio points to whoever the scene is about. The topic is the subject of the opening sentence; if that subject is a pair, it is whichever of the two is first named on their own as a subject. Naming someone else later does not move the topic.
  2. The single object pronoun. Since the reflexive needs a doubled pronoun, a single koi never points back to its own subject. With two people in play, it must be the other one.
  3. This one and that one. When two people are introduced together, kio i tse o (that one) is the one named first and kio i tse a (this one) is the one named second, like English "the former" and "the latter." The reference is fixed from that point on; it does not shift with each new mention.
  4. Repeat the name or noun. When everything else runs out, say the name again, as speakers of Turkish and Persian do.

Two people, neither the topic

The hardest case is two people of the same number with nothing to tell them apart. English fails here too: "Erena and Iortan had a falling-out. She ran off, and she followed her. When she caught sight of her, she apologized to her, and she let it go." Nobody could follow that. Ionnoioni can:

  2. kio i tse a tas strosoi. This one, the one named second, fled: Iortan.
  3. sne Erena tas tro o koi. Erena is the first of the pair named on her own as a subject, so she becomes the topic. A single koi cannot be its own subject, so it is the other one: Erena walked after Iortan.

One name repeated, and every pronoun has exactly one reading. Where English needs names at every turn, Ionnoioni needs only one.

Guidelines for Coining New Words

These are reasoning tools that have emerged from real coinage sessions, not fixed rules. They describe patterns in how new words have actually been built, offered as guidance for future coinage rather than as grammar a learner needs to master.

Compound ordering tendency

In most existing compounds, a grounding or core element tends to come first, with a qualifying or active element following it. This is an enthusiastic suggestion, not a codified rule, it describes a pattern that keeps emerging, not a requirement every compound must satisfy.

𑖝𑖲𑖂𑖂𑖌𑖡𑖿
tuiion
body/self+being
human being, self grounds the word before "being" folds into it
𑖡𑖿𑖃𑖎𑖿𑖃
noikoi
being+alarm+self
shame, being is the ground; alarm-turned-to-self is the event happening to it

Standalone descriptive roots, not forced into a family

When a new concept doesn't fit cleanly into an existing root family's vowel-slot system, or when forcing it in would collide with a word already claimed by that family, the better move is often to coin a standalone descriptive root instead. It attaches to whatever noun it modifies through the ordinary attributive particle i, the same way numerals do, rather than needing a dedicated grammatical slot of its own.

𑖡𑖿𑖨𑖺𑖎𑖺 𑖂 𑖝𑖿𑖭𑖰
nroko i tsi
strangeATTRtree
a strange tree

This is how a naming collision between kai (child) and an early proposal of kai (totem) was resolved: rather than disturb the kau (animal) vowel-shift family to find it a new slot, totem was re-coined as kopsi (self + marker/monument), a free-standing word with no dependency on kau's vowel system at all.

𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖊 𑖎𑖺𑖢𑖿𑖭𑖰 𑖂 𑖎𑖻
nio e kopsi i kau
1SGPOSStotemATTRanimal
my totem-animal

Escalation through bracket grammar, not separate coinages

When several related concepts sit on a single continuum of intensity or severity, the existing bracket particles (ra = intensify, ta = soften) can generate the whole range from one base root, rather than requiring a new word at every point on the scale.

𑖝 𑖡𑖝𑖲𑖂
ta natui
SOFTdisconnection.MOOD
mild, numbness, muted but present
𑖨 𑖡𑖝𑖲𑖂
ra natui
INTENS(onset)disconnection.MOOD
building, the felt sense of self or surroundings starting to slip
𑖨 𑖡𑖝𑖲𑖂 𑖨
ra natui ra
INTENSdisconnection.MOODINTENS
full, identity-level fragmentation, intense throughout

One coinage, natui (nan "zero/void" + tui "physical self"), carries an entire spectrum through bracketing alone, four distinct, usable meanings from a single root. The same economy applies to the voluntary/involuntary particles above: a single word like tsopro (catastrophizing) or krutsor (rumination) carries its own default reading, with tr or pr added only when the distinction needs to be made explicit.

A dedicated page, "Idioms, Metaphors & Kennings," covers the companion discipline: when to build an image instead of coining an atomic word, and when to leave that image unfused rather than compress it.

A language that expects dialects

In the standard language, each word begins cleanly. A vowel particle keeps its own syllable even beside another vowel, so a u noi (with us) is spoken as three words, not as au (pain) followed by noi, and ka o (what for) stays apart from kao (community). Siddham shows the same thing on the page, since a vowel that begins a word is written with its full glyph.

That is a description of the standard, not a fence around it. Ionnoioni has already changed under its speakers' tongues: nokotu (perhaps) is nroko i trun worn down by fast speech, and tasopar (time) is tas o par fused through use. Places where words lean into one another are exactly where a community of speakers might take the language somewhere new, letting a u run together and finding a meaning of their own in it.

Other directions are just as natural. A group might turn i before another vowel into a full y (ya, ye, yo, yu) and u before a vowel into w or v, which is what Sanskrit sandhi, the home tradition of Siddham, has always done, and write them with 𑖧 and 𑖪. Another might prefer b to p, or d to t, and reach for 𑖤 and 𑖟. The letters are already waiting in the script.

None of this would be getting Ionnoioni wrong. A dialect is a sign that a language is alive, and anyone who takes it in a new direction is doing exactly what this guide invites: helping it grow.
