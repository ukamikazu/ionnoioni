Ionnoioni

Idioms, Metaphors & Kennings

Ionnoioni's closed phonemic inventory of six consonants and five vowels keeps the language small on purpose. When a new concept arrives and no root quite reaches it, the first instinct is never to mint a new atomic word. It is to build an image out of words already there: a kenning. This page is about that discipline, why it exists, how it works and the small, real decisions that separate a good kenning from a lazy one.

Why kennings exist at all

The simple-root length ceiling (four segments) exists to keep ordinary vocabulary compact and learnable. But some concepts are not simple. A heart is not one idea, it is an organ, a location, a rhythm and a seat of feeling. Forcing that into a four-segment atomic root would either lose the idea's real shape or demand an arbitrary new sound with no internal logic at all. Kennings are the deliberate exception: compounds built from existing roots that are allowed to exceed the length ceiling, because their whole purpose is carrying more than one root can hold alone.

This is not merely permission to compound freely. An ordinary compound (trekra, "give safe" = care for) fuses cleanly into a new, single working word. A kenning stays visibly made of its parts. The seams are the point: a kenning is understood by seeing what it is built from, not just by knowing what it means.

The kennings on record

prin tron saisau, heart

"The small dark rhythm." Built from prin (small) + tron (dark) + saisau (rhythm), three roots, not two, explicitly exceeding the ordinary compound ceiling. An early candidate, pse (tool), was rejected: a heart is not wielded the way a tool is, it animates, so framing it as an instrument misstated the whole relationship. This kenning is also explicitly provisional: it stands in for "heart" until a proper anatomy vocabulary (distinct organs, distinct from tui, the body generally) gets its own session. Kennings can be placeholders as honestly as they can be permanent.

𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖊 𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖰𑖡𑖿 𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖺𑖡𑖿, 𑖊 𑖭𑖹𑖭𑖻 𑖡 𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖲
nio e prin tron, e saisau na pru
1SGPOSSsmall-dark(heart)POSSrhythmNEGknow.V
my heart, its rhythm is uncertain

kre rna, excitement / anticipation

"Good dawn." Built from kre (good) + rna (dawn, the day-cycle word for the very start of the day, already carrying an open, forward-moving sense from its own grammar). Rather than coin an abstract word for excitement, the feeling is anchored to a time of day that already means beginning.

𑖝𑖰𑖌 𑖝𑖿𑖭𑖺𑖨𑖿 𑖝𑖰𑖌 𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖻 𑖝 𑖎𑖻𑖡𑖿 𑖝 𑖌 𑖭𑖿𑖝𑖸 𑖎𑖿𑖨𑖸 𑖨𑖿𑖡 𑖎?
tio tsor tio prau ta kaun ta o ste kre rna ka?
2SGbelieve.V2SGheavy.VSOFTgriefSOFTtoward+or(instead of)good+dawn(excitement)Q
you think you're heavy with sadness instead of excited?

tso pru, proof

"The way toward knowing." Built from tso (way/manner) + pru (know), left deliberately unfused, two visible words rather than one compressed root. This is the clearest example on record of a kenning kept open on purpose: proof is the path to certainty, not certainty itself, and the sentence that uses it enacts that path as it goes, rather than handing over a finished abstraction.

𑖍 𑖝𑖿𑖭𑖺 𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖲 𑖂 𑖎𑖿𑖨𑖸
au tso pru i kre
painway+know(proof)ATTRgood
pain, proof-path, bound to good (the pain is proof of how good it was)

kaoreo, fellowship

"Community-with-outsiders." Built from kao (community, one's own people) + reo (outsider-group, other people). The tension in the compound is deliberate: a fellowship is a real community, one you are granted entry into, but it begins as reo, foreign, before it can become kao. The word carries the bittersweetness of the thing it names rather than smoothing it into something purely positive.

tsupsatei / printsutronpsatei, moss

Two kennings for the same thing, at two different registers. tsupsatei (water+stone+plant) is the working, everyday form, compact and immediately parseable. printsutronpsatei (small+wet+dark+rock+plant) is a five-root kenning reserved for poetic or literary register, the same concept rendered with more texture and weight than daily speech needs. Not every concept wants two forms, but when a word carries real poetic potential, nothing stops it from having both a plain and an ornamented version.

tia tau teu / Tiatauteu, Earth

"Beloved elder garden." Built from tia (beloved), tau (elder) and teu (garden, kept animate because gardens are living places). The planet is named as something loved, older than us and alive. In everyday speech the three words run together into Tiatauteu, while the open form stays for weightier speech.

pe i psa / pipsa, basis

"The stone below." Built from pe (down) and psa (stone), joined by i. Every house, argument and life rests on a stone beneath it, so the kenning covers a foundation, the grounds of an argument and a starting point alike. In fast speech the e and i blur together and the kenning wears down to pipsa, the everyday word. The same psa is the counting stone and the coin, and pipsa is also the name of the alphabet song, The Basis.

Two ways to touch a kenning

Once a kenning exists, two different particles let you use it, and they do different jobs. This distinction only became visible once a kenning (prin tron saisau, heart) needed to be used in two different ways inside the same conversation.

i: the kenning as one whole

i binds the kenning as a single, indivisible unit, the same general binding job i does everywhere else in the grammar. No piece of the kenning is singled out; the whole compound is treated as one idea and a quality or clause attaches to it directly. This is the compact, everyday form.

𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖊 𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖰𑖡𑖿 𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖺𑖡𑖿 𑖭𑖹𑖭𑖻 𑖂 𑖡 𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖲
nio e prin tron saisau i na pru
1SGPOSSsmall-dark-rhythm(heart)ATTRNEGknow.V
my heart-rhythm is uncertain

e: reaching inside a kenning

e's ordinary job is possession. Used a second time inside a sentence, it can reach into a multi-root kenning and re-possess just one inner piece of it, rather than claiming the compound as a whole. This decomposes the kenning in a way i never does. It is reserved for a more creative or technical register, where the parts of the image matter individually, not only the image as a whole.

𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖊 𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖰𑖡𑖿 𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖺𑖡𑖿, 𑖊 𑖭𑖹𑖭𑖻 𑖡 𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖲
nio e prin tron, e saisau na pru
1SGPOSSsmall-dark(heart)POSSrhythmNEGknow.V
my heart, its rhythm is uncertain

Neither form is more correct than the other. They are different registers: i takes the image whole; e opens it up and speaks to one of its parts.

Reaching for a metaphor instead of a missing word

Sometimes the right move is not a kenning at all, it is refusing to name the literal thing and naming what it was actually standing in for. Nothing in Ionnoioni names "groceries" or "a store." Rather than coin either, a worry about not knowing a new city became a sentence about trusting one's own competence in an unfamiliar place.

𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖲 𑖝𑖿𑖭𑖸 𑖎𑖿𑖨𑖸 𑖝𑖿𑖭𑖺 𑖎𑖿𑖨 𑖝𑖿𑖭𑖺𑖨𑖿 𑖡𑖿𑖃 𑖡𑖿𑖃
pru tse kre tso kra tsor noi noi
know.Vspace/placegoodwaysafe+believe(trust)1SG.OBJ1SG.OBJ
I know the place well enough to trust myself

The literal English detail (which store sells what) never gets built at all. What survives is the feeling the detail was carrying: not yet trusting your own footing somewhere new. The missing word produced a truer sentence than filling the gap would have.

Threading a reserved word through an idiom

nan, zero, carries a deliberate in-world weight, a reserved void-word. "From scratch" borrows that weight on purpose, threading nan through the existing "away from" construction (na o) rather than coining a separate phrase for starting with nothing.

𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖲𑖡𑖿 𑖎𑖌 𑖂 𑖝𑖸𑖄 𑖡 𑖌 𑖡𑖡𑖿
trun kao i teu na o nan
cause.VcommunityATTRgardenNEGtowardvoid
building the community-garden, away from void (from scratch)

This is the same discipline as the kennings above, reach for what already exists and carries the right weight, rather than inventing something new. The payoff here is thematic: the same word anchoring the whole numeral system does quiet, poetic work in an ordinary sentence about starting something new.

Textured time: a literary register

The S class has a subclass called material color, which places a single vowel in front of an inanimate root: s + a + kso (fire) gives sakso, a hearth. The same shape fits the three time words that begin with rs: rsa (evening), rso (dusk) and rsu (night). Everything these words say can already be built in ordinary speech, so they are not everyday vocabulary. They belong to a literary register, for poetry and prose, the same way the long moss kenning does.

As with material color, the time word grounds each word and the leading vowel qualifies it.

What they can do in a line

Because these words share their shapes so closely, they lend themselves to devices that ordinary vocabulary does not:

  • A night in three words. sorsu, sursu, soirsu: nightfall, the dead of night, night terrors. One root carries the whole line, and only the leading vowel moves.
  • Descending through the day. sorsa, sorso, sorsu: evening drawing in, the light failing, nightfall. This time the vowel holds still and only the root changes, so the line itself grows darker.
  • Near-echoes. saursu (a sleepless, aching night) can be heard against saur (a lingering charge), and soirsu (night terrors) sits close to stronsoi (fear of the dark). In everyday speech these would be ambiguities; in a poem they are resonances.

More devices like these will likely turn up as the S class is explored further.

When to build a kenning, and when not to

  • Reach for a kenning when the concept genuinely has internal structure. A heart is an organ, a location and a rhythm at once; "proof" is a path, not a static fact. If a concept only makes sense as more than one idea stacked together, a kenning is honest about that. A concept that is really just one simple idea doesn't need one.
  • Decide early whether it should stay unfused or compress. tso pru stayed two visible words because the sentence needed to show proof being built. tsopro (catastrophizing) compressed hard because it needed to become an ordinary, frequently-reached-for word. There is no single rule here, only the question: does this concept want to be seen as a process, or does it want to become a word people stop noticing the seams of?
  • Check whether the concept might not need new words at all. The groceries example above is the strongest case for this: sometimes the honest move is translating the feeling behind a missing word rather than the word itself.
  • A kenning can be provisional. prin tron saisau stands in for heart until real anatomy vocabulary exists. Marking a kenning as provisional, rather than quietly treating it as final, keeps the door open to a more precise word later without pretending the placeholder was never a placeholder.
  • Reuse a reserved or thematically loaded word when one exists. nan already carried void-weight before "from scratch" needed it. Reaching for an existing word's connotation does more work than coining something neutral.
