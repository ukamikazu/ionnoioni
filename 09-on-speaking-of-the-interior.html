On Speaking of the Interior

A framework for discussing feelings, emotions and states. A work in progress.

This page is not a phrasebook. It will not hand you a list of stock lines to memorize for a difficult conversation. Instead it tries to explain a framework: what Ionnoioni offers for talking about the interior life that English does not, why each piece was built the way it was, and how to actually reach for it when the moment calls for it.

None of this is clinical advice, and none of it claims any special authority over real human feeling. It is simply an attempt to build a language with room enough to hold what is often hard to say.

1. The Foundation: Mood Roots

Ionnoioni has a whole grammatical class dedicated to feeling: mood roots, built from pure vowel pairs with no consonant at all. They are not verbs describing an emotion from the outside. Under the zero-copula rule, a mood word placed after a subject simply is the predicate:

toi ai "you are desire." You want, you reach, you seek

The three founding primals come from Jaak Panksepp's cross-species affective neuroscience, the actual biological primitives researchers use, not folk categories borrowed secondhand from English:

WordSiddhamCore affect
ai𑖋desire / SEEKING
oi𑖃alarm / FEAR
au𑖍pain / PANIC-GRIEF

Everything in this page builds outward from these three, or from the small additional set of full primals (rage, lust, care, play) added once the core three were in place. The point of starting here is this: feeling, in Ionnoioni, is not an adjective bolted onto a sentence. It is a state of being, stated directly.

2. Marking How a Feeling Arrives

Two particles, ra (intensify) and ta (soften), bracket a mood root to mark its shape over time rather than just its presence. Placement carries real information:

PatternMeaning
ra [root]sudden onset
[root] rabuilding, resolving outward
ra [root] raintense throughout
ta [root] tasustained, low-grade unease

This is not decoration. English has no clean grammatical way to say "this feeling arrived suddenly" versus "this feeling has been building" without a whole extra sentence. Ionnoioni marks it in two syllables, and critically, delivery (whispered, shouted, flat) completes the meaning. The grammar gives the shape; the voice gives the weight. Neither one is written down as a separate note. This is deliberate: feeling is not fully captured on a page, only pointed at.

ra oi ra unrelenting alarm. Shouted, this is shock; whispered, it is insidious dread

3. The Shape of Transition

A number of words exist specifically for the space between one state and another, the coming-down, the settling, the quiet after. English tends to flatten all of these into "I'm fine now" or "I'm better." Ionnoioni keeps them separate:

WordSiddhamMeaning
aies𑖋𑖊𑖭𑖿afterglow
oiroi𑖃𑖨𑖿𑖃calm after fright
saur𑖭𑖻𑖨𑖿lingering charge, not yet settled
aues𑖍𑖊𑖭𑖿grief settling toward quiet

If someone asks how you are doing three weeks after a loss, "aues" says something true and specific that "I'm okay" does not: not gone, not raw, settling.

4. Grief, Memory and Birth

Two small clusters sit opposite each other, one built on a k/n consonant frame for death and memory, the other on the reversed n/k frame for birth. The mirroring is deliberate: these are understood as structurally related thresholds, not unrelated topics.

WordSiddhamMeaning
kaun𑖎𑖻𑖡𑖿grief
kain𑖎𑖹𑖡𑖿dread of death
koun𑖎𑖺𑖄𑖡𑖿the departed, spoken of
koin𑖎𑖿𑖃𑖡𑖿haunting unease, the uncanny feeling of a continued absence
WordSiddhamMeaning
nain𑖡𑖹𑖡𑖿wonder of birth

Notice that grief (kaun) and the uncanny feeling of someone's lingering absence (koin) are not the same word, and are not expected to arrive at the same time. A person can move from one to the other, or hold both, and the language gives each its own name rather than collapsing them into one undifferentiated "sad."

5. Shame: Alarm Turned Inward

Coining a word for shame took real hesitation. Shame does measurable harm, and there was a genuine wish that it simply had no place in how people are expected to feel. But a language spoken by people living in the actual world does not make a feeling disappear by declining to name it. It only makes the feeling harder to locate and examine once it arrives. Naming a feeling is not the same as endorsing it, any more than the existence of a word for rage endorses rage.

The resolving insight: shame is alarm at the self. Not fear of an external threat, but oi (alarm) turned reflexively inward.

𑖡𑖿𑖃𑖎𑖿𑖃 noikoi shame. n (being) + oi (alarm) + k (the self-referential element found in the kio/koi pronouns)

Because it takes the full bracket grammar, noikoi can mark onset, too: ra noikoi for a sudden wave of shame, noikoi ra for shame that has been quietly building.

6. Ambivalence: Wanting Inside Uncertainty

Ambivalence is not two opposing feelings canceling each other to nothing. It is two pulls held at once, neither resolving, neither winning. The word that captures this grew out of an existing compound, na pru (not-know, already glossed as uncertain), fused with ai (desire):

𑖡𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖹 naprai ambivalence. Wanting something while uncertain about the wanting itself

This is a more honest shape for ambivalence than two emotions colliding. Most ambivalence is not "I feel love and hate in equal measure." It is closer to: I want this, and I do not know what wanting it means right now.

7. Dissociation: A Spectrum, Not Four Separate Words

Clinically, dissociation is not one feeling. It covers a real cluster: depersonalization (detachment from one's own self, watching from outside), derealization (the world feels unreal, distant, dreamlike), dissociative amnesia (gaps in memory or continuity) and, at the far end, a fragmented or discontinuous sense of identity. None of this is the same as simple numbness, a flattening of feeling. Dissociation is better understood as a disconnection, a severed wire rather than a lowered volume, and it is widely understood as a protective response to what would otherwise be overwhelming.

Rather than coin four unrelated words, these were treated as points on a single continuum, the existing bracket grammar doing the work of marking severity on one root:

𑖡𑖝𑖲𑖂 natui base root. nan (zero/void) + tui (the physical self): "zero physical self"
PatternMeaning
ta natuimild numbness, feeling muted but present
natuithe base disconnection state
ra natuibuilding, the felt sense of self or surroundings starting to slip
ra natui rafull, identity-level fragmentation

Dissociative amnesia needed no new word at all. It composes directly from existing vocabulary:

𑖡 𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖲𑖡𑖿 𑖎𑖲𑖌 na prun kuo not-remember the whole-self. A gap in the self's own continuity, not ordinary forgetting

8. Speaking From Before Words

Sometimes there is nothing to compose yet. Ionnoioni keeps a register for exactly this: unbroken runs of vowels, carrying no grammar, attaching to no sentence. Length and arc carry meaning, not the specific vowels chosen. A short cry is a short cry; a long, unbroken run is something closer to being overwhelmed.

This register can stand alone, or be dropped into the middle of an ordinary sentence as a kind of phonetic punctuation, skipped over by the grammar around it. A small set of shapes have become conventional through use, not fixed vocabulary, but familiar gestures:

ShapeSiddhamConventional sense
ia𑖂𑖀surprise, sharp onset into open release
ea𑖊𑖀anger, a sharp call
aue𑖍𑖊plaintive, pleading
uea𑖄𑖊𑖀raw grief, soft sobbing
eia𑖊𑖂𑖀exasperation

Why this matters for actually speaking of the interior: it gives a real, legitimate place to put "I don't have words for this yet." Not a failure to speak, not stepping outside language, but a recognized register within it.

9. Putting It Together

A worked sentence, using several pieces from this page at once. Someone describing the weeks after a loss, grief settling, haunted by absence and occasionally slipping into the mild end of disconnection:

𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖎𑖻𑖡𑖿, 𑖭𑖿𑖡𑖸 𑖎𑖿𑖃𑖡𑖿 𑖝, 𑖭𑖿𑖡𑖸 𑖝 𑖡𑖝𑖲𑖂 𑖝 nio kaun, sne koin ta, sne ta natui ta I grieve, and there is a haunting unease, a little, and I am a little numb, a little

Nothing here is a fixed phrase to be copied. The point is the method: reach for the mood root closest to what is actually happening, bracket it for how it is arriving, and let silence (the primal register) stand in honestly wherever naming would be false precision.

Further gaps will surface with use, the way shame, ambivalence and dissociation surfaced this session, and will be added here as they are worked through.

10. A Worked Dialogue

Everything above is vocabulary and principle. This section is a single sustained test: an exchange modeled on a cognitive-behavioral therapy session, working through the pattern of mistaking a feeling for evidence (sometimes called emotional reasoning). The goal was not just to translate a conversation, but to see whether the language's existing grammar, built for other reasons entirely, would carry real therapeutic nuance without needing to invent a special register for it.

What's doing the work here

Mood as predicate, not description. Every oi in this exchange is a full predicate under the zero-copula rule, not an adjective describing the client. "I am alarm" is a structurally different kind of claim than "I feel alarmed", there is no distance between the speaker and the state, which is exactly the thing the therapist is working to introduce.

ra...ra versus bare roots. The client's panic carries the full intensity bracket, ra oi ra, throughout the opening exchange. By the final line, the alarm is still named honestly (nothing here pretends the feeling vanished) but it has shifted to ta saur, a softened, lingering-charge mood rather than raw, bracketed alarm. The grammar tracks the client's actual state turn by turn, rather than flattening it to "better now."

kuo as the reframe's anchor. The English original reaches for a borrowed, slightly technical metaphor: emotions are not data collectors. Ionnoioni already had a better-fitted word sitting unused: kuo, the whole person as accumulated body, mind and history. "Alarm is not kuo" says something the English metaphor only gestures at, a feeling is not the sum of who you are, not because feelings don't record facts, but because kuo was never separable into that kind of ledger to begin with.

raeu, built live. No word for "a dangerous place" existed going into this exchange. Rather than invent a new root, rae (enemy, a person) took the existing containment particle u as a compound, not a vowel-shift, raeu, a place immersed in threat. rae itself is untouched; the new word sits beside it. This is ordinary compounding, the same mechanism behind trekra and noikoi, simply applied in the moment a real gap appeared.

The echo in the final correction. The therapist's key reframe quietly repeats the client's own opening claim, word for word, now bracketed inside a negated cause-clause: na trun [nio par kre na], "it does not cause [I will not succeed]." The client's exact panicked sentence becomes the object the therapist shows is not caused by the alarm. Nothing this precise was planned in advance; it fell out of reusing trun (cause) honestly.

This dialogue is a test, not a script. The claim is not that this is the only way to say any of this in Ionnoioni, but that the grammar built for entirely different reasons over the preceding weeks turned out to carry real psychological nuance without strain, without a dedicated "therapy register," and without borrowing a single metaphor from English that didn't already have a better-fitted native equivalent.

11. A Second Dialogue: Holding Two True Things at Once

The first dialogue tested correcting a distortion, a feeling wrongly treated as proof of an outcome. This one tests the opposite problem: two feelings that are both entirely true at the same time, with no distortion to fix and nothing to resolve. Alex has just learned they got a fellowship across the country. Jordan is a close friend, not a therapist, and the closeness itself matters: the intimacy particle nr appears here between two friends rather than inside a clinical alliance.

What's doing the work here

The inverse of Dialogue 1's lesson, stated in the grammar itself. In the first dialogue, oi trun toi tsor (alarm causes you to believe) was the error being corrected: feeling wrongly treated as proof. Here, Alex says au tso pru i kre, pain is proof of good, and this time the feeling genuinely is legitimate evidence. The difference is marked structurally, not just by context: no causal particle (trun) appears. Pain and the-proof-of-goodness are bound together with i, co-occurring, linked, rather than claimed as cause and effect. The two dialogues reach opposite conclusions using the same grammar, told apart by one particle.

Two ways to touch a kenning. prin tron saisau (heart, literally "the small dark rhythm") gets handled two different ways in one exchange. Alex reaches inside it and re-possesses just one piece: e prin tron, e saisau na pru, "my heart, its rhythm is uncertain," each noun separately claimed with e. A few lines later the same compound is bound as one whole unit with i: prin tron saisau i na pru. Neither is more correct than the other; they are different registers, one reaching into a kenning's structure, one taking it as a single idea.

Metaphor standing in for missing vocabulary, on purpose. Nothing in Ionnoioni names "groceries" or "a store." Rather than coin either, Alex's worry about not knowing the city becomes pru tse kre tso kra tsor noi noi, "I know the place well enough to trust myself." The literal English detail (which store sells what) is dropped in favor of what it was actually standing in for: not yet trusting your own competence somewhere new. The gap produced a truer sentence than filling it would have.

na o nan, reaching for the reserved word. "From scratch" is built by threading nan, the language's reserved zero/void word, through the existing "away from" construction: na o nan, away from void. Starting a community garden "from nothing" literally begins at the void and moves away from it, the same word that anchors the whole numeral system doing quiet, poetic work in an ordinary sentence.

Forgetting, not merely leaving. Alex doesn't say the garden's rhythm will be left behind; they say na prun koi e saisau, "forgetting its rhythm." The mechanism of loss is memory fading, not distance, which is a sadder and more honest claim than simple departure would be.

A pronoun left deliberately unresolved. kao i teu binds community and garden into one phrase, "community-garden," before koi (its) refers back to it. koi carries no marking that would say whether "its" means the community's or the garden's specifically, and here that's treated as accurate rather than broken: the rhythm belonged to the whole fused thing, not separably to either half.

par on a mood word for the first time. tio ta par kaun ta marks grief as pointed at the future, anticipatory grief for something not yet lost, by placing the ordinary future-tense particle directly on a mood root rather than a verb. Nothing about par's original job required this, but nothing forbade it either.

Another speaker, another word. Alex calls the feeling in their chest sesa, a heavy knot, which is exactly what the English source says. Material color offers a rival: saupsa, a stone on the heart. Neither is more correct. As with Jordan's skronsa beside Alex's kre rna, the choice says something about the speaker: a knot is something tangled that might still be undone, while a stone is simply carried.

Where the first dialogue showed the grammar correcting a distortion, this one shows it holding two things open at once without forcing either to resolve, which is arguably the harder and more honest thing language is asked to do.

12. A Third Dialogue: From Armor to Texture

The first dialogue showed a client flooded by a single undifferentiated feeling, and the second held two true feelings open at once. What's doing the work here

Who owns the texture words. Elena uses none of her own in her first two speeches; she talks in plain verbs, kinship terms and clock time. Every early texture word comes from Dr. Vance: skaursi, stronsai. In her third speech she finally uses texture words, but only as weapons aimed at him (skrisoi, tsorse) or denied outright (na stronsai). Only after her defenses crack does she name her own: raur, skronsau, spraisai, then skransu. The last texture word in the dialogue, strise, belongs to both of them.

Four roots that run the whole length. pro (hold): the stone mind she holds, the space he holds, the wreckage she is left holding, the mother who never held, and at the end no longer holding. kri (see): "she never saw me," "she will see me," and the therapist who does see her. tso (way): her procedure, her mother's pattern, the way she does not know how to live. tran (decide): deciding for her mother, the child deciding for herself, and finally no need to decide.

One phrase, two readings. When Elena says nio na pru tso, the words first meant "I don't know the way." Since pru tso (knowing the way) is also how Ionnoioni says "can," the same line reads "I can't." Both are true of her at that moment, and the language says them at once.

Never, twice. Elena says her mother nan rne kri noi, never saw her, literally "zero mornings saw me." Dr. Vance answers in her own word: the pain tio e koa nan rne pro, your mother never held. Because nan rne is a function word rather than a count, it is spelled out in Siddham instead of being written with the digit ०.

Now. Dr. Vance's last speech opens with tasapar, now. tasopar, past toward future, is time as it flows; tasapar, past with future, is the one point where they touch. It is the same pairing as here and there, tse a and tse o, and in weightier speech it opens out into tas a par.

Here and there. tse a (space with) is "here," and Dr. Vance uses it for the room itself: tse a, a noi, here, with me. Its partner tse o is "there," so an earlier draft of line 8, nio pro tse o tio e au, could be heard as "I hold there your pain." Material color resolved it: satse (a room, a nook) gives nio pro satse o tio e au, "I am holding a room for your pain," which is closer to what a therapist means by holding space, with no overlap left to sort.

Gentle to the end. Dr. Vance's last words bracket strise (comfortable silence) with ta on both sides: u nio par ta strise ta nr, a quiet that stays soft all the way through.

Two small kennings, one family. tsa i psa (stone mind) is the fortress of intellect, and tsa i tron (dark mind) is the hidden mind beneath it. Clinical terms are rendered by what they mean rather than what they are called: the repetition compulsion is krukra, which already names the safety-seeking function, and "the withholding object will choose you" becomes simply kio par kri toi, she will see you.

A curse, a name and two numbers. kso! (fire!) is an inanimate root fired off as an oath, using the ks- sound color for profanity. Elena's name becomes Erena, since the inventory has no l. And in Siddham, "four hours" and "you are one" are written with the digits ४ and १, as every number is.

Read beside the first dialogue, this one completes a pair. One client had too much feeling and not enough words to tell it apart; this one had the words walled away. Between them, they show what a vocabulary of texture is for.
