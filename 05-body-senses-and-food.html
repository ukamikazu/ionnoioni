Body, Senses & Food

This page gathers the words for the body, the senses and food. Nearly all of them grow from one small new class, the intake class, and from two ways of building on it: the outside and the inside of the body, and the tool made for each act. Most of the vocabulary is recycled from roots the language already had.

The intake class

Every other class either opens on a consonant or is made of vowels alone. The intake class opens on a vowel, closes the mouth twice in the middle and opens again: vowel, two consonants, vowel. The two consonants fall in different syllables (ap.na, ak.pa), so the shape is easy to say without any extra vowel. Open, close, open is the shape of a bite and a swallow, and every word in the class names something crossing from the world into the body.

The eight consonant pairs fall into two kinds. The three that release through the nose (pn, kn, tn) name the channels of the air: breath, hearing and smell. The five double closures (pk, pt, kp, tk, tp) name the channels of the mouth. With a on both sides, each pair is a verb.

Outside and inside

Body parts are named for what they do. A function plus tui (the physical body, the outside we can see) names the part you can see, and everyday speech wears tui down to -ti. A function plus kuo (the whole person, inwardness and felt experience) names the part inside, and with it the sense as it is felt. Not every act needs both.

The two forms sound different as well as meaning different things. A -ti word leans on its first syllable (AK-na-ti, ear). A -kuo word is heavier at the back, and its stress moves inward (ak-NA-kuo, hearing as felt). Nobody designed this: the inside words carry their stress inward on their own. It is a tendency to listen for, not a rule to mark.

The heart

The heart has a family of its own, built on saisau (rhythm).

WordSiddhamMeaning
saisauko𑖭𑖹𑖭𑖻𑖎𑖺the heart, the organ that completes each beat
saisaukuo𑖭𑖹𑖭𑖻𑖎𑖲𑖌the pulse felt from within, the heartbeat pounding in the chest; also the heart as felt
saisauti𑖭𑖹𑖭𑖻𑖝𑖰the pulse as an outward sign, found at the wrist or throat

saisauko is the one word that keeps the worn-down -ko, and its stress falls at the very end: sai-sau-KO. The rhythm runs through its two diphthongs and then lands, the way a heartbeat completes its circulation. The poetic name for the heart, the kenning prin tron saisau (the small dark rhythm), stays for poetry. A plain cry of being alive is simply saisauko!

Intimate anatomy

These words are plain and ungendered, one for every body.

WordSiddhamBuilt fromMeaning
aiti𑖋𑖝𑖰desire + bodygenitals
aikuo𑖋𑖎𑖲𑖌desire + insidethe inner reproductive organs; also desire as felt
trekrati𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖸𑖎𑖿𑖨𑖝𑖰nurture + bodybreasts
triti𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖰𑖝𑖰settle + bodybuttocks

Ionnoioni has no words for man or woman, and its pronouns carry no gender. Following Turkish, which has one neutral word for the sexual organ and adds a qualifier only when clarity needs it, Ionnoioni marks biological sex with two qualifiers joined by i. Biology defines the sexes by the gamete: one is small and moves toward the other, and the other stays where it is. The pair follows the particle senses o (toward) and i (fixed).

WordSiddhamMeaning
ost𑖌𑖭𑖿𑖝𑖿male: the moving gamete (aiti i ost, the male organ)
ist𑖂𑖭𑖿𑖝𑖿female: the still gamete (aiti i ist, the female organ)

The two are an exact pair, equal in weight, and neither is built from the other. They describe bodies, and animals as readily as people (nau i ost, a tomcat). kio e aiti is "their genitals," with no gender anywhere in it.

Food

Foods live in the intake class's other vowel frames. The pair names the channel: pk for food, pt for drink, tk for taste. The lead vowel names the source, with the same moving and still logic as ost and ist: i the rooted thing (plants), o the moving thing (animals), u the water-dweller and e what people make between them. The trailing vowel names the form: -a a single piece, -i small standing bits, -u soft, many or steeped, -e something passed between.
