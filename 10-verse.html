Ionnoioni: all pages

Ionnoioni

Verse

Ionnoioni sounds light. Its syllables are open, its five vowels are always given their full value, its stops are quick taps and its r rolls. Spoken aloud it has a bouncing, almost sung gait, and its heavier words, the hissing textures and the clipped clusters, stand out against that brightness. This page gathers what the language has shown so far about making verse: the line, alliteration, rhyme, the riddle, a stanza taken from the alphabet itself, four styles and the seeds that can stand beside a poem.

The line

The verse line is borrowed from Old English: two half-lines with a pause between them, each carrying two strong beats. Content words take the beats. The little words, i, e, a, o, na and the pronouns, fall into the dips between them, and since the language is full of them the line always has room to breathe. Words alliterate across the pause, and the second half alliterates on its first beat, never its last.

Each poem below carries a scansion under its romanized lines for readers who want it: ' marks a beat, ˘ a dip and ‖ the pause. A two-syllable word is written as one unit, such as '˘ for iptu. Vowels side by side count as separate syllables (ni-o, ti-a, ku-o), except the diphthongs ai, au and oi, and a syllabic r carries a syllable of its own (krp, rsu). Most words lean on their first syllable, while inside words carry their stress inward.

When one-syllable content words pile up, every one of them insists on a beat. That is a fault in a calm line and a gift in an urgent one: an overloaded half-line is the heart pounding too hard for the meter, as the second line of the riddle below shows.

Alliteration

In most languages alliteration is only sound. In Ionnoioni the word families share their openings, so a run of alliterating words also carries the color of its sound: a line beating on pr- is serious business, a line beating on tr- is light, and a line hissing on ks- is close to an oath. Any vowel alliterates with any other, as it did in Old English, which lets the primals and the intake words chime together.

Poets have also noticed that words beginning or turning on rs tend to belong to endings: evening, dusk and night, sleep, the moon and the stars, the west. It is an observation for now, not a rule.

Rhyme

Full rhyme comes almost too easily. Most words end on one of five open vowels, so pra, kra and tra rhyme, and so do pro, tso and kso. The art lies in slant rhyme, and the language offers four kinds:

  • Vowel steps. Neighboring vowels nearly rhyme, e with i and o with u: pro and pru, tre and tri, tis and tes.
  • Primal tinges. A plain vowel against its diphthong, so the rhyme lands slightly bruised by desire, pain or alarm: tra and trau, kra and krai, pro and proi.
  • The nasal tail. An open word against its twin closed by n, like a door shutting: kri and krin, tre and tren, tro and tron.
  • The guttering s. A texture against its lengthened grade, the s trailing off: sesa and sesas, sasa and sasas.

The riddle

The Old English riddles speak in the first person and often end by daring the listener to say what the speaker is called. Ionnoioni has no word for a name, so its riddles end the way its speakers ask: how do they call it?

The Basis: a stanza from the alphabet

The alphabet song is called pipsa, The Basis, and its four phrases give a stanza of four lines:

  • The cry. Three quick beats and a breath, like ai au oi.
  • The open line. Five broad, even beats, like a e o i u.
  • The working line. Clipped pairs with catches between them, like na-ka, ta-pa, sa.
  • The landing. A single word, held, like ra.

The last two phrases of the song end on sa and ra, so the stanza closes on a rhyme or near-rhyme when the poet wants one.

Four styles

The rules of a poetic tradition should fit on a note card. Each style here has three guidelines, one for sound, one for rhythm and one for structure, and nothing more. Three short guidelines leave an enormous space to play in. The Basis sits under all of them as the root stanza: it lands on ra, held. Three of the styles land in their own ways, and the fourth refuses to land at all.

Realism: the krikuo

Realism alone falls flat in a language with so small a concrete vocabulary. Its answer is a form called krikuo, after the word for the mind's eye, built from kri (see) and kuo (inside). Every line but the last holds only what can be seen, counted or timed: no mood words, no texture words, no kennings. The last line is a single texture word, the only interior in the poem.

  1. Plain. Every line but the last holds only what can be seen, counted or timed. The strictness is a favor to the poet, since unadorned facts are what make the last word land.
  2. Short statements. One fact to a clause, each one stopped where it ends.
  3. One turn inward. The poem lands on a single texture word, the only interior in it.

Nominalism

Nominalism is made of names: texture words, mood words and the time words set side by side, often with no verb at all. Sound leads the way, and the meaning comes from the naming itself, word placed against word. The two styles mirror each other. Realism is all particulars with one name at the end, and Nominalism is all names, sometimes ending on one plain fact.

  1. Names in a row. Texture words, mood words and time words, with sound leading the way.
  2. Side by side. Word is set against word with little or no verb, and the zero copula does the joining.
  3. The verdict. Lines are propositions and the poem reasons like a syllogism, closing each claim on ers or irs, true or false.

Every line begins on rs and ends on rs. The day closing is true. The moon going down is false, since it only seems to leave. And a star's death is true, because the light we see can come from a star already gone.

Antichisism

The name comes from the Greek word for reverberation, a reverberating echo. In Antichisism one consonant cluster runs through the line: it opens the first word, sits inside the middle word and closes the last, so the sound is heard arriving, passing through and going out.

  1. Echo. One cluster opens the first word, sits inside the middle word and ends the last word.
  2. Breath. Particles such as e, nr, tr and pr may be added as dips so the line can breathe. Whether a dip takes its own beat or runs into the next word is the performer's choice, and it is never written.
  3. Return. Lines come in threes, and the third answers the first.

Here the cluster is sp: it opens sprausai (melancholy), sits inside tispe (snow) and ends isp (thin). The first line breathes with dips, and the second splits into two half-lines.

Dynamism

Realism and Nominalism are both poetry of things and names. Dynamism breaks with them on purpose: it is poetry made of verbs, and where the other styles come to rest, it keeps moving.

  1. The verbs drive. The stop and r openings of the verbs lead, and the trill rolls the line forward.
  2. Momentum. Verbs follow verbs, lines may run on, and a verb may be said twice.
  3. No landing. The poem ends on a verb still in motion and closes on ta, released and not stopped.

Teikai: seeds

A poem can be set with a teikai (𑖝𑖸𑖂𑖎𑖹), a seed, the plant's child: a single sound, usually a consonant cluster, written large beside the lines it grows from. The practice is borrowed from the raga. Before a raga is performed the musician explores it slowly and without meter, so the listener is inside its mode before the piece begins, and under the whole performance a drone keeps sounding. A seed does both. Before reciting, the performer sounds the seed and settles into it, and how that is done is never written. On the page the seed is written large and the lines are set no higher and no lower than it.

Where the seed stands changes how the eye moves through the poem, and each of the four styles has found its own place. The four placements have borrowed names, reshaped to fit the language.

Arap: the seed above, for Antichisism

Arap (𑖀𑖨𑖢𑖿) comes from the Hindustani ālāp, the slow opening of a raga, with the l made r since Ionnoioni has none. The seed sits above the poem with open space around it. At the end of the last line nothing stops the eye, so it drifts back up to the seed and into the first line again. Antichisism is made to loop: here the last word ends on sp and the first word opens on it, so the end of the poem hands it back to its beginning.

Antapi: the seed below, for Realism

Antapi (𑖀𑖡𑖿𑖝𑖢𑖰) comes from the Sanskrit antādi, end and beginning, the name of a Tamil form whose verses link end to start. The p keeps it clear of the body ending -ti. The seed is the sound that begins the poem's last word, set at the very end. The eye falls through the plain lines to the seed, finds its match one line up and glances back to it once before it settles, the way a dropped thing bounces before it comes to rest. The krikuo's one interior word is read twice.

The seed stands at the left, before the lines, like the figure beside a geometric proof, and each line is a step that ends on its verdict. In this poem every line opens and closes on rs, so the seed is sounded again inside every claim.

Karnan: the seed after, for Dynamism

Karnan (𑖎𑖨𑖿𑖡𑖡𑖿) comes from karman, the grammarian Pāṇini's word for the object of an action, which also simply means a deed. The m becomes n, as it does in neter. Ionnoioni puts the object after the verb, so a seed set to the right becomes the object of every verb in the poem. At the end of each line the eye goes to the seed before it can go on, a pause the seed grants. Here the seed is tr, the particle for what is done of one's own accord, and the poem never reaches it.

The pull toward a verdict

Ionnoioni has no copula and no tense, so a bare line reads like a plain statement, and it has two words, ers and irs, that are simply true and false. Its verse keeps drifting toward a shape of two statements followed by a verdict, even when nothing asks for it. Whether that shape is a form or only a habit is for future poets to decide.
