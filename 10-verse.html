Ionnoioni: all pages

Ionnoioni

Verse

Ionnoioni sounds light. Its syllables are open, its five vowels are always given their full value, its stops are quick taps and its r rolls. Spoken aloud it has a bouncing, almost sung gait, and its heavier words, the hissing textures and the clipped clusters, stand out against that brightness. This page gathers what the language has shown so far about making verse: the line, alliteration, rhyme, the riddle, a stanza taken from the alphabet itself and two styles.

The line

The verse line is borrowed from Old English: two half-lines with a pause between them, each carrying two strong beats. Content words take the beats. The little words, i, e, a, o, na and the pronouns, fall into the dips between them, and since the language is full of them the line always has room to breathe. Words alliterate across the pause, and the second half alliterates on its first beat, never its last.

When one-syllable content words pile up, every one of them insists on a beat. That is a fault in a calm line and a gift in an urgent one: an overloaded half-line is the heart pounding too hard for the meter, as the second line of the riddle below shows.

Alliteration

In most languages alliteration is only sound. In Ionnoioni the word families share their openings, so a run of alliterating words also carries the color of its sound: a line beating on pr- is serious business, a line beating on tr- is light, and a line hissing on ks- is close to an oath. Any vowel alliterates with any other, as it did in Old English, which lets the primals and the intake words chime together.

Poets have also noticed that words beginning or turning on rs tend to belong to endings: evening, dusk and night, sleep, the moon and the stars, the west. It is an observation for now, not a rule.

Rhyme

Full rhyme comes almost too easily. Most words end on one of five open vowels, so pra, kra and tra rhyme, and so do pro, tso and kso. The art lies in slant rhyme, and the language offers four kinds:

  • Vowel steps. Neighboring vowels nearly rhyme, e with i and o with u: pro and pru, tre and tri, tis and tes.
  • Primal tinges. A plain vowel against its diphthong, so the rhyme lands slightly bruised by desire, pain or alarm: tra and trau, kra and krai, pro and proi.
  • The nasal tail. An open word against its twin closed by n, like a door shutting: kri and krin, tre and tren, tro and tron.
  • The guttering s. A texture against its lengthened grade, the s trailing off: sesa and sesas, sasa and sasas.
𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖎𑖿𑖨𑖰, 𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖎𑖿𑖨𑖰𑖡𑖿
𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖝𑖿𑖨, 𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖻
nio kri, nio krin
nio tra, nio trau
I see, I read
I am at ease, I am struck
A nasal tail and then a primal tinge. Each pair almost rhymes, and the meaning turns in the gap.

The sun's fire goes away,
the ember's fire settles with me.

My beloved gives me tea:

Many stars, and the moon is one,
my beloved and I are two.
Three couplets. The first alliterates on the fire sound ks- and rhymes slant (o against noi), the second turns to t- and tr- and rhymes in full, and the third alliterates on r- and closes on two bare numerals: the moon is one and the two of us are two.

The riddle

The Old English riddles speak in the first person and often end by daring the listener to say what the speaker is called. Ionnoioni has no word for a name, so its riddles end the way its speakers ask: how do they call it?

𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖰𑖡𑖿 𑖂 𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖺𑖡𑖿 ‖ 𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖺𑖡𑖿, 𑖢𑖿𑖨𑖺 𑖎𑖲𑖌
𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖻, 𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖻, 𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖻 ‖ 𑖡 𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖰, 𑖡 𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖺
𑖎 𑖎𑖰𑖌 𑖄 𑖎𑖰𑖌 𑖢𑖿𑖨 𑖎𑖿𑖃?
prin i tron ‖ pron, pro kuo
trau, trau, trau ‖ na tri, na tro
ka kio u kio pra koi?
Small and dark, I live, holding the self.
Hit, hit, hit, never resting, never walking.
How do they call it?
The answer (open once you have guessed)

saisauko, the heart. The clue is planted in its kenning, prin tron saisau, the small dark rhythm, and the three blows of trau are the heartbeat itself.

The Basis: a stanza from the alphabet

The alphabet song is called pipsa, The Basis, and its four phrases give a stanza of four lines:

  • The cry. Three quick beats and a breath, like ai au oi.
  • The open line. Five broad, even beats, like a e o i u.
  • The working line. Clipped pairs with catches between them, like na-ka, ta-pa, sa.
  • The landing. A single word, held, like ra.

The last two phrases of the song end on sa and ra, so the stanza closes on a rhyme or near-rhyme when the poet wants one.

The sun comes up,
my beloved gives me water.
Drink, taste: good.

Line two can also be heard as a request, "Beloved, give me water," since Ionnoioni marks no imperative and needs no subject.

The consonant line falters: n and k have no word to give, and only t, p and s answer.

Two styles

Realism: the krikuo

Realism alone falls flat in a language with so small a concrete vocabulary. Its answer is a form called krikuo, after the word for the mind's eye, built from kri (see) and kuo (inside). Every line but the last holds only what can be seen, counted or timed: no mood words, no texture words, no kennings. The last line is a single texture word, the only interior in the poem.

८𑖎𑖿𑖨𑖿𑖢𑖿, ३०𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖿𑖎𑖿। 𑖝𑖿𑖭𑖲𑖢𑖸।
𑖝𑖰𑖀 𑖎𑖿𑖨𑖻 𑖊𑖢𑖿𑖎 𑖌 𑖝𑖡𑖿।
𑖡𑖰𑖌 𑖀𑖢𑖿𑖝। 𑖭𑖿𑖝𑖿𑖨𑖰𑖭𑖸।
ton i krp, kan seo i trk. tsupe.
tia krau epka o tan.
nio apta. strise.
Eight thirty. Rain.
My beloved cuts the bread in two.
I drink. Comfortable silence.

Nominalism

The other style is made of names: texture words, mood words and the time words set side by side, often with no verb at all. Sound leads the way, and the meaning comes from the naming itself, word placed against word. The two styles mirror each other. Realism is all particulars with one name at the end, and Nominalism is all names, sometimes ending on one plain fact.

𑖨𑖿𑖭 𑖭𑖺𑖨𑖿𑖭 𑖊𑖨𑖿𑖭𑖿
𑖨𑖿𑖭𑖲𑖎𑖿𑖭𑖹 𑖢𑖨𑖿𑖭𑖺 𑖂𑖨𑖿𑖭𑖿
𑖨𑖿𑖭𑖲𑖎𑖿𑖭 𑖭𑖿𑖎𑖻𑖨𑖿𑖭 𑖊𑖨𑖿𑖭𑖿
rsa sorsa ers
rsuksai parso irs
rsuksa skaursa ers
Evening, evening drawing in: true.
The moon in the west: false.
A star, one bounded death: true.
Every line begins on rs and ends on rs. The day closing is true. The moon going down is false, since it only seems to leave. And a star's death is true, because the light we see can come from a star already gone.

The pull toward a verdict

Ionnoioni has no copula and no tense, so a bare line reads like a plain statement, and it has two words, ers and irs, that are simply true and false. Its verse keeps drifting toward a shape of two statements followed by a verdict, even when nothing asks for it. Whether that shape is a form or only a habit is for future poets to decide.
